# Risk context

- PWH are at higher risk of HPV infection and premalignant cervical lesions compared to patients who are HIV-negative
- Premalignant cervical lesions are common in PWH
- The risk of cervical cancer is elevated approximately 3- to 5-fold in PWH
- Persistent infection with high-risk HPV leads to the development of cervical cancer

# Screening, DHHS guidance

- DHHS has established guidance for cervical cancer screening in PWH
    - See the Human Papillomavirus section of Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Opportunistic Infections in Adults and Adolescents with HIV
- Begin with cervical cytology at the time of HIV diagnosis for PWH aged 21 years or older
- Co-testing for high-risk HPV can be considered for PWH aged 25 years or older
    - Transient HPV is highly prevalent before age 30 years
    - This may increase the likelihood of unnecessary colposcopy in those aged 25 to 29 years
- Cytology normal
    - Repeat cytology is recommended, with timing dependent on HPV testing results
- Cytology ASCUS
    - High-risk HPV testing can be considered if not already performed
    - Positive for high-risk HPV, evaluate by colposcopy
    - HPV testing negative or not performed, repeat cervical cytology in 6 to 12 months
- Cytology low- or higher-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion
    - Colposcopy is recommended

# Screening, WHO guidance

- In contrast, the WHO recommends HPV DNA testing as the primary screening modality in both PWH and the general populations
- For those who test positive for HPV, the WHO guidance includes
    - Partial genotyping
    - Colposcopy
    - Visual inspection with acetic acid
    - Or cytology

# Treatment of premalignant cervical lesions

- Treatment of these lesions is generally safe and effective regardless of HIV status
- Endocervical extension is more frequent among PWH
    - Loop excision is less effective, with higher recurrence rates in PWH than in patients without HIV
- For HSIL, excision (preferred) or ablative procedures such as cryotherapy are recommended
    - Per the American Society of Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP)-sponsored consensus guidelines for management of cervical cancer screening abnormalities
    - Endorsed by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and other organizations
- HPV-based surveillance is also recommended
- Even with screening and treatment, there are high rates of progression of HSIL to cancer in the general population
- For recommendations regarding preventative HPV vaccination in PWH, see NCCN Guidelines for Prevention and Treatment of Cancer-Related Infections

# Lymphadenopathy

- Non-malignant causes for lymphadenopathy should be considered in PWH
- Suspicious PET-avid lymphadenopathy should be biopsied, to differentiate between nodal metastasis and infectious etiology
    - Consult with an HIV specialist
    - If negative for cancer, refer for an infectious disease workup

# Field effect of HPV oncogenesis

- PWH with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) or invasive cervical cancer should also be evaluated for field effect of HPV oncogenesis
    - Anal cancer, see NCCN Guidelines for Anal Carcinoma
    - Vulvar cancer, see NCCN Guidelines for Vulvar Cancer (Squamous Cell Carcinoma)

# Cervical cancer treatment in PWH

- Refer to an HIV specialist to ensure the patient is on an effective ART regimen
- Treat for cervical cancer as per the NCCN Guidelines for Cervical Cancer
    - Including use of concurrent chemotherapy for patients receiving definitive radiation treatment
- Modifications to cancer treatment are not recommended based solely on HIV status
- Poor performance status may be from HIV, cancer, or other causes
    - The reason for poor performance status should be considered when making treatment decisions
    - ART is recommended for all PWH and may improve performance status
- Drug interactions can occur in patients with cervical cancer and HIV
    - Both an HIV pharmacist and an oncology pharmacist should be consulted
    - See Principles of Systemic Therapy and Drug-Drug Interactions (HIV-B)

# If an immune checkpoint inhibitor is used

- Evolving data suggest that ICIs are generally safe and effective in PWH, with the following considerations
- Concurrent KSHV infection, there may be increased risk of KSHV-associated inflammatory syndromes such as MCD or KICS when ICIs are used
    - If the patient has a history of KSHV-associated diseases, consider more frequent monitoring of signs and symptoms of KICS or MCD
    - Unexplained fevers should prompt workup of MCD and KICS with
        - C-reactive protein
        - KSHV serum viral load
        - SPEP
        - IL-6
        - IL-10
    - The diagnosis of KICS requires excisional biopsy of lymphadenopathy to exclude MCD
- The use of ICIs has been linked to TB reactivation and flare of mycobacterial disease
    - Screening for latent TB infection can be considered before administering an ICI
    - So that the patient can be monitored for TB symptoms and treatment of mycobacterial infection can be expedited
