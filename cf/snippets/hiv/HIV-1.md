# Anal cancer screening in PWH

- PWH are at higher risk of premalignant anal epithelial changes compared to patients who are HIV-negative
- PWH diagnosed with penile, vulvar, vaginal, or cervical disease should have screening for anal cancer
- Annual anal cancer screening, recommended by both IANS (Stier EA, et al. Int J Cancer 2024) and DHHS (Human Papillomavirus section of Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Opportunistic Infections in Adults and Adolescents with HIV) although the details of their recommendations differ
    - MSM with HIV aged 35 years or older
    - Transgender women with HIV aged 35 years or older
    - Other PWH aged 45 years or older
- Screening methods
    - Anal cytology
    - High-risk human papillomavirus testing
    - Digital anorectal exam (DARE)
- High-resolution anoscopy (HRA) should be performed where available if any of the following, per IANS, per Palefsky JM, et al. N Engl J Med 2022, and per the DHHS opportunistic infections guidelines
    - Anal squamous intraepithelial lesions identified by cytology
        - High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL)
        - Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL)
        - Atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASCUS)
    - Anal symptoms
    - Abnormal examinations
- If HRA capacity is limited, high-risk HPV testing is recommended to help identify those at highest risk to be prioritized for HRA
- Standard anoscopy is recommended if HRA is not available

# Treatment of anal dysplasia

- Multiple methods are available
    - Topical therapy, ie, fluorouracil, imiquimod
    - Excision
    - Ablation
- These treatments are safe in PWH and are associated with a decreased risk of progression to cancer, even though treatment of anal dysplasia in PWH is associated with a high risk of recurrence (Palefsky JM, et al. N Engl J Med 2022)
- DHHS Guidelines consider office-based hyfrecation, a form of electrocautery ablation, as a reasonable first-line treatment of HSIL, since this treatment was used most frequently in the ANCHOR trial
- In a randomized controlled trial of PWH who engage in receptive anal intercourse, electrocautery (ablation) was better than topical therapy, although recurrence rates were still high (Richel O, et al. Lancet Oncol 2013)
- Even with screening and treatment, there are high rates of progression of HSIL to cancer in PWH, so prevention of HPV acquisition and of subsequent anal HSIL is important
    - For preventative HPV vaccination, see NCCN Guidelines for Prevention and Treatment of Cancer-Related Infections

# Anal cancer in PWH

- PWH have an approximately 15- to 20-fold increased likelihood of being diagnosed with anal cancer compared with individuals who are HIV-negative
- Anal cancer accounts for approximately 5%-10% of cancers diagnosed in PWH
- Anal cancer in PWH is often associated with persistent anal HPV infection
- HPV-related disease in PWH is often multifocal, so PWH diagnosed with anal cancer should be evaluated for the presence of vulvar, vaginal, or cervical disease
- Non-malignant causes for lymphadenopathy should be considered in PWH
    - Biopsy suspicious PET-avid lymphadenopathy to differentiate nodal metastasis from infectious etiology, consult with HIV specialist
    - If negative for cancer, refer for an infectious disease workup
- Co-manage with an oncologist and an HIV specialist
- Modifications to cancer treatment should not be made solely based on HIV status
- Anal cytology can be considered for detection of anal dysplasia in survivors of anal cancer with HIV, although its value in detection of recurrent anal cancer is limited
- People who engage in receptive anal intercourse should discuss post-treatment pelvic physical therapy and anal dilators with an appropriate health care provider
- Poor performance status in PWH and anal cancer may be from HIV, cancer, or other causes
    - Consider the reason for poor performance status when making treatment decisions
    - ART is recommended for all PWH and may improve performance status
- Drug interactions can occur in patients with anal cancer and HIV
    - Consult both an HIV pharmacist and an oncology pharmacist
    - See Principles of Systemic Therapy and Drug-Drug Interactions (HIV-B)

# Immune checkpoint inhibitors in PWH

- Evolving data suggest that immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) are generally safe and effective in PWH, with the following considerations
- Concurrent KSHV infection may carry increased risk of KSHV-associated inflammatory syndromes when ICIs are used
    - Multicentric Castleman disease (MCD)
    - KSHV-associated inflammatory cytokine syndrome (KICS)
    - If the patient has a history of KSHV-associated diseases, consider more frequent monitoring of signs and symptoms of KICS or MCD (Polizzotto MN, et al. Clin Infect Dis 2016; Uldrick TS, et al. JAMA Oncol 2019)
    - Unexplained fevers should prompt workup of MCD and KICS
        - C-reactive protein
        - KSHV serum viral load
        - Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP)
        - Interleukin (IL)-6
        - IL-10
        - Diagnosis of KICS requires excisional biopsy of lymphadenopathy to exclude MCD
- ICI use has been linked to opportunistic infections, including tuberculosis (TB) reactivation and flare of mycobacterial disease
    - Screening for latent TB infection can be considered before administering an ICI, so the patient can be monitored for TB symptoms and treatment of mycobacterial infection can be expedited
