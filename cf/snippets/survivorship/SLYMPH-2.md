# What lymphedema is

- Potential side effect after cancer treatment resulting from damage to the lymphatic system
- Approximately 3 in 4 cases diagnosed within 3 years of treatment
    - Can develop anytime in the life of the survivor
- Can be acute or chronic, depending on stage of diagnosis
- Can impact any area of the body (eg, arms, legs, face, trunk, groin)

# Symptoms

- Swelling on the same side as the cancer treatment is a universal symptom
- Additional initial symptoms may include
    - Sensation of heaviness
    - Fatigue
    - Fullness or tightness in the skin
    - Pain
- Later-stage symptoms may include
    - Decreased range of motion or function
    - Thickening of the skin

# Who is at risk

- Surgery, radiation, or chemoradiation to the axillary, supraclavicular, cervical, or pelvic inguinal lymph node system
- Sentinel node biopsy also increases risk, although less than complete dissection
- Risk raised by
    - BMI ≥30 kg/m2
        - Weight management may be considered when appropriate for individuals with obesity
    - Localized infection
    - Increased number of nodes removed
    - Higher initial extent of disease

# Baseline and screening

- Obtain pretreatment baseline objective measurements when possible, for patients with treatment-related or individual risk factors
    - Validated volumetric tools, such as perometry, 3D camera, or tape measure/girth measures (may be converted to volumetric measures)
    - And/or tools that measure extracellular fluid, such as bioimpedance spectroscopy
- Early detection/diagnosis and early referral are key for optimal management
    - Stages 0 and 1 are reversible
    - Stages 2 and 3 are less responsive to treatment
- Regularly screen survivors at risk for lymphedema by
    - Symptom assessment
    - Clinical exam
    - Validated volumetric tools and/or tools that measure extracellular fluid
- Educate patients about early symptoms and signs, including fullness, tightness, heaviness, and pain
- Lymphedema may cause or exacerbate psychological distress (SANXDE-1)

# Infection risk

- Survivors at risk for lymphedema and those with lymphedema are at higher risk of localized infection in the affected area
    - Can require hospitalization for IV antibiotics
- Educate survivors with or at risk for lymphedema to inform their medical provider immediately for signs of infection in the affected area

# Exercise

- Progressive weight training under supervision and physical activity are not associated with exacerbation or development of lymphedema

# Precautionary procedures

- Observational studies show air travel, venipuncture, and blood pressure measurement (via arm cuff) are not associated with development of lymphedema
    - Precautionary measures likely unnecessary
    - More research needed on the effect of these procedures on lymphedema risk
- In the absence of high-level data, Panel recommends venipuncture and blood pressure measurement be done on the non-at-risk limb; if necessary, may be done on the at-risk limb
- Similar studies have not been conducted in individuals with lymphedema
- Panel recommends avoidance of venipuncture and blood pressure measurement (via arm cuff) in any limb with lymphedema
    - Patients with bilateral upper extremity lymphedema should consult their physician to explore safe alternatives for venipuncture and blood pressure monitoring, to minimize risk of exacerbating swelling
        - Strategies may include using smaller-gauge needles, avoiding tourniquets, and selecting alternative sites for blood pressure measurement or blood draws
