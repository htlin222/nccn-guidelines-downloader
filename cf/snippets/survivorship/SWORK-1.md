# General principles

- Discuss work needs before treatment begins, so treatment recommendations can take work needs into consideration if possible
- Symptoms affecting work may wax and wane with treatment or disease status
    - Especially relevant if living chronically with cancer, or with the consequences of cancer treatment
    - Some survivors continue working through treatment
    - Others may start and stop working more than once
- Assess for underemployment and work limitations due to cancer or treatment side effects, not only unemployment or failure to return to work during active treatment

# Financial and psychosocial impact

- Employment helps protect survivors from financial toxicity
    - In the United States, employment is frequently tied to health insurance access
    - May be a reason survivors work even when not fully recovered
- Financial toxicity is common among cancer survivors
    - Consider screening and referral to financial counselor and/or social worker as early as possible
- United States does not have federally mandated paid sick leave for survivors or their caregivers
    - Absence of paid sick leave may affect adherence to treatment regimens and surveillance
    - May affect ability to maintain employment during treatment
- Employment is an important source of personal interaction, normalcy, and social support
    - Psychosocial effects/advantages of work may include: sense of purpose, emotional well-being, link to identity, improved quality of life, connection with others, distraction

# Populations at increased risk for work-related difficulties

- Risk factors: gender, age, race, ethnicity, cancer type, cancer stage, rural residence, educational attainment, job type and demands, work environment
- Increased difficulty more likely for survivors with
    - Physically or cognitively demanding jobs
    - Jobs with limited flexibility in scheduling or tasks
- Consider potential for discrimination as a result of diagnosis/illness in decisions surrounding employment

# Counseling and accommodations

- Offer information to help survivor understand their likely ability to work
    - Take into account finances and personal/family needs
    - Discuss potential work accommodations with employer (ADA Guide to Disability Rights Laws)
- Regularly re-evaluate work-related concerns post active cancer treatment, or periodically for persons living chronically with cancer
    - Periodically identify goals and barriers regarding work with survivor (SWORK-3)
    - Team approach may be needed
    - Consider early involvement of social work, primary care, physical therapy/occupational therapy, cancer rehabilitation, and/or career counseling services, if available
- Employment disability forms not typically well-suited to cancer
    - Consider survivor's needs for flexibility in tasks and hours
    - Consider other workplace accommodations as a starting point for filling out necessary forms
