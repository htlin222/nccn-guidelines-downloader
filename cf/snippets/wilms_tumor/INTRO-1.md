# Care team

- Comprehensive care by a multidisciplinary team with experience in managing renal tumors
    - Team led by a pediatric oncologist
- WT is largely a disease of children, though adults may occasionally present with high-risk disease
    - Treatment for adults with WT is similar to treatment for pediatric patients
    - Referral to, or partnership with, a pediatric oncologist familiar with the treatment of WT is recommended
- On this page the terms male and female refer to sex assigned at birth

# Epidemiology

- WT accounts for 5% of childhood cancers
- Most common primary renal tumor in children
    - Accounts for >90% of renal tumors in patients <20 years
- Five-year survival is >90% with appropriate treatment
    - Outcome of some groups, particularly diffuse anaplastic WT (DAWT), remains poor
- Incidence is highest among African American children, followed by white children
    - Children of Asian descent have the lowest incidence
- More than 75% of WT present between 1–5 years, most commonly 3 years
- Most patients have a solitary tumor at presentation
    - 5%–13% have bilateral tumors
    - 10% have multifocal tumors in a single kidney
- Median age at diagnosis for unilateral tumors is 35 months for males and 42 months for females
- Median age at diagnosis for bilateral tumors is 23 months for males and 28.5 months for females

# Clinical presentation

- Abdominal distention and/or presence of an abdominal mass (83%)
- Abdominal pain (37%)
- Fever (23%)
- Hematuria (21%–25%)
- Hypertension (20%–25%)
- Less common symptoms
    - Varicocele
    - Hernia
    - Enlarged testicle
    - Congestive heart failure
    - Hypoglycemia
    - Cushing syndrome
    - Pleural effusion
    - Acute abdomen
- Almost 10% of patients with WT have coagulopathy (acquired von Willebrand disease)

# Distinguishing WT from neuroblastoma

- A healthy-appearing child with an abdominal mass is more likely to have WT
- A child with neuroblastoma tends to be ill-appearing at presentation
- Calcification of the tumor appears in approximately 5%–10% of WTs
    - Versus approximately 60%–70% of neuroblastomas

# Sites of spread

- Lung (81%)
- Lung and liver (15%)
- Other (4%)
- Spread to regional lymph nodes (LNs) also occurs

# Genetic predisposition

- WT is associated with genetic predisposing conditions in 10% to 15% of cases
    - Some reports suggest >30% of cases
    - See WILMS-I (2 of 5)
- Denys-Drash syndrome (male pseudohermaphroditism and glomerulopathy)
- WAGR syndrome (WT, Aniridia, Genitourinary abnormalities, and a Range of Intellectual Disability)
- Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (macroglossia, hemihyperplasia/lateralized overgrowth and umbilical hernia)
- Perlman syndrome
- Frasier syndrome
- Sotos syndrome
- Simpson-Golabi-Behmel syndrome
- Bloom syndrome
- Li-Fraumeni syndrome
- Trisomy 18
- Associated physical findings
    - Aniridia is present in 1% of children with WT
    - Hemihyperplasia appears in 2%–3% of patients with WT
    - Genitourinary malformations are found in 5% of patients with WT
        - Cryptorchidism
        - Hypospadias
        - Fused (horseshoe) kidneys

# Screening if a predisposing condition is present

- Routine screening for WT is recommended
    - Physical exam (PE) every 3 months
    - Kidney ultrasound (US) every 3 months
    - Continue until 7 years of age, ie, all of year 6
- Children with multifocal/bilateral disease present at a younger age than children with unilateral disease
    - They are often identified as part of a surveillance program for patients with a predisposing condition
