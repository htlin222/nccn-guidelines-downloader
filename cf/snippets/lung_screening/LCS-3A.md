# CT technique and follow-up process

- All screening and follow-up chest CT scans should use a CT dose index volume (CTDIvol) threshold of not more than 3 mGy for an individual of average size (o)
    - Exception: evaluating mediastinal abnormalities or lymph nodes, where standard-dose CT with IV contrast might be appropriate (LCS-A)
    - Adjust parameters for individuals of smaller or larger size
    - There should be a systematic process for appropriate follow-up
    - See ACR-STR Practice Parameter for the Performance and Reporting of Lung Cancer Screening Thoracic Computed Tomography (CT)
- Use a tracking tool, in addition to a navigation process (p)
    - So that individuals with screen-detected lung nodules complete guideline-concordant nodule management
    - So that individuals without significant screen-detected abnormalities continue annual screening if they remain eligible
- Ideally the annual LDCT is performed 12 months from the initial or interval scan (r)
- If intervening CT scans are done, they may be used to reset the time schedule of ongoing lung cancer screening follow-up (v)
- There is uncertainty about the appropriate duration of screening and the age at which screening is no longer appropriate (u)

# Nodule definitions and measurement

- These guidelines are harmonized with Lung-RADS, with rounding of mean measurement to the nearest whole number in mm (q)
- A nodule is a 3D opacity, measuring up to 3 cm in diameter (t)
    - Solid nodule: homogeneous soft-tissue attenuation
    - Nonsolid nodule, also known as a ground-glass nodule: hazy increased attenuation that does not obliterate bronchial and vascular margins
    - Part-solid nodule: elements of both solid and nonsolid nodules
- Evaluate and measure nodules on the LDCT lung windows (t)
    - The size of all nodules is underestimated when viewed on soft-tissue windows
    - Some nodules may not even be visible on soft-tissue windows, particularly nonsolid nodules and small nodules
- Report the measurement as the average diameter rounded to the nearest whole number (w)
    - Mean diameter is the mean of the longest diameter of the nodule and its perpendicular diameter
    - For round nodules only a single diameter measurement is necessary

# Before applying the solid nodule pathway

- The pathway assumes none of the following are present (s)
    - Benign pattern of calcification
    - Fat in nodule suggestive of hamartoma
    - Features suggesting inflammatory etiology
- When multiple nodules or other findings are present that suggest occult infection or inflammation is a possibility, suggest follow-up LDCT in 1-3 months (s)

# Limitations of FDG-PET/CT

- FDG-PET has a low sensitivity for nodules with <8 mm of solid component (x)
- FDG-PET has a low sensitivity for small nodules near the diaphragm (x)
- FDG-PET/CT is only one consideration of multiple criteria for determining whether a nodule has a high risk of being lung cancer (x)
- In areas endemic for fungal disease, the false-positive rate for FDG-PET/CT is higher (x)

# Determining suspicion of lung cancer

- The evaluation for the suspicion of lung cancer requires a multidisciplinary approach with expertise in lung nodule management (y)
    - Thoracic radiology
    - Pulmonary medicine
    - Thoracic surgery
- May include use of a lung nodule risk calculator to assist with probability determination (y)
    - Mayo risk model
    - Brock university model
    - Model by Herder GJ, et al.
    - The use of risk calculators does not replace multidisciplinary nodule management
    - Geographic and other factors can substantially influence the accuracy of nodule calculators

# Tissue diagnosis

- Tissue samples need to be adequate for both histology and molecular testing (z)
- If biopsy is non-diagnostic and a strong suspicion for cancer persists, suggest one of the following (aa)
    - Repeat biopsy
    - Surgical excision
    - Short-interval LDCT follow-up, 3 months
- See the diagnostic evaluation of a lung nodule (DIAG-1 through DIAG-A) in the NCCN Guidelines for Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (bb)
- Individuals with a strong probability of peripheral stage I lung cancer, based on risk factors and radiologic appearance, do not require a biopsy before surgery (cc)
    - A biopsy adds time, cost, and procedural risk and may not be needed for treatment decisions
    - A preoperative biopsy may be appropriate for a central nodule or mass
    - A preoperative biopsy may be appropriate if a non-lung cancer diagnosis is strongly suspected, which can be diagnosed by bronchoscopy, core biopsy, or fine-needle aspiration (FNA)
    - A preoperative biopsy may be appropriate if an intraoperative diagnosis appears difficult or very risky
    - When a preoperative tissue diagnosis has not been obtained, an intraoperative diagnosis, that is wedge resection or needle biopsy, is necessary before lobectomy, bilobectomy, or pneumonectomy
    - See Principles of Diagnostic Evaluation in the NCCN Guidelines for Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer
- Prior to treatment, multidisciplinary evaluation is required (dd)
    - Includes treating physicians and specialists in obtaining tissue diagnosis: thoracic surgery, pulmonology, and interventional radiology
    - Determines feasibility, along with the safest and most efficient approach for biopsy
    - Or provides consensus that a biopsy is too risky or difficult, that a clinical diagnosis of lung cancer is appropriate, and that treatment is warranted

# Treatment note

- SABR is also an appropriate option for individuals with high surgical risk (ee)
    - This should include a multidisciplinary evaluation, including at least thoracic surgery and radiation oncology
    - See Principles of Radiation Therapy in the NCCN Guidelines for Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer
