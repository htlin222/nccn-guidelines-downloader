# Family history and germline risk

- Family or personal cancer history, and/or family or personal history of high-risk germline variants, can inform when to begin shared decision-making regarding prostate cancer early detection
- Suspicious family history
    - Should undergo genetic testing
    - See NCCN Guidelines for Genetic/Familial High-Risk Assessment: Breast, Ovarian, Pancreatic, and Prostate (CRIT-1)
    - See NCCN Guidelines for Genetic/Familial High-Risk Assessment: Colorectal, Endometrial, and Gastric (LS-1)
- Known or suspected cancer susceptibility gene
    - Referral to a cancer genetics professional is recommended
    - Germline variants in prostate cancer risk genes may carry an elevated lifetime risk of prostate cancer
    - Certain variants also carry an elevated risk of early-onset and/or potentially lethal prostate cancer (eg, BRCA2)
    - For susceptibility genes and cancer risk management based on genetic testing results, see GENE-A and BRCA-A
- Genetic testing does not show a germline variant in a prostate cancer risk gene, but family history is concerning
    - Shared decision-making regarding the timing and frequency of PSA testing

# Black/African American individuals

- Significantly higher incidence of prostate cancer, earlier age at diagnosis, and increased prostate cancer mortality compared to white individuals
    - The higher incidence and earlier age at diagnosis are attributable to a greater incidence of preclinical prostate cancer and a higher likelihood that a preclinical tumor will spread
- Survival disparities relate to these factors and to social determinants of health, lack of access to care, and treatment disparities
    - Not to race itself, and not to differences in the frequency of tumor-acquired genomic variants
    - Black patients who receive equitable prostate cancer treatment have similar outcomes to white patients
- Need for higher vigilance in this population, in the form of appropriate screening and treatment
- Data show that Black individuals benefit more from screening than white individuals
    - Little evidence that screening at an earlier age decreases morbidity and mortality compared to testing at age 45
    - Earlier screening may increase overdiagnosis
- Many support the recommendation to consider beginning shared decision-making about PSA screening at age 40 years
- Many support considering screening at annual intervals rather than every other year

# Medications and exposures

- 5α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride and dutasteride) are known to decrease PSA by approximately 50%
    - Correct PSA values accordingly in patients taking these medications
- Exposure to Agent Orange (eg, many Vietnam War veterans)
    - Inform the patient that Agent Orange exposure may be associated with an increased risk of high-grade prostate cancer

# PSA and DRE

- The best evidence supports the use of serum PSA for the early detection of prostate cancer
- DRE should not be used as a stand-alone test
- DRE can be considered as a baseline test in addition to serum PSA in all patients
    - Greatest usefulness in those with elevated PSA
- Consider referral for biopsy or further testing if DRE is suspicious for cancer at any PSA level
