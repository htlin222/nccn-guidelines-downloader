# History and physical

- Family cancer history
    - Family or personal cancer history, and/or family or personal history of high-risk germline variants, can inform when to begin shared decision-making regarding prostate cancer early detection
    - A suspicious family history should prompt genetic testing (CRIT-1, LS-1)
    - If genetic testing does not show a germline variant in a prostate cancer risk gene but family history is concerning, use shared decision-making regarding the timing and frequency of PSA testing
- Family or personal history of high-risk germline variant
    - If there is a known or suspected cancer susceptibility gene, referral to a cancer genetics professional is recommended
    - Germline variants in prostate cancer risk genes may carry an elevated lifetime risk of prostate cancer, and for certain variants an elevated risk of early-onset and/or potentially lethal prostate cancer (eg, BRCA2) (GENE-A, BRCA-A)
- History of prostate disease and cancer early detection
    - Prior PSA and/or isoforms
    - Prior exams
    - Prior biopsies
- Black/African American identity
    - Significantly higher incidence of prostate cancer, earlier age at diagnosis, and increased prostate cancer mortality compared to white individuals
    - Survival disparities relate to these factors and to social determinants of health, lack of access to care, and treatment disparities, not to race itself
    - Many support beginning shared decision-making about PSA screening at age 40 years, and considering screening at annual intervals rather than every other year
- Medications
    - 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (finasteride and dutasteride) are known to decrease PSA by approximately 50%
    - Correct PSA values accordingly in patients taking them
- Environmental exposure
    - Inform patients with exposure to Agent Orange (eg, many Vietnam War veterans) that it may be associated with an increased risk of high-grade prostate cancer

# Risk assessment, when to begin

- Age 40 to 75 y for:
    - Black/African American individuals
    - Those with germline variants that increase the risk for prostate cancer, see NCCN Guidelines for Genetic/Familial High-Risk Assessment: Breast, Ovarian, Pancreatic, and Prostate
    - Those with concerning family or personal history
- Age 45 to 75 y for all others
- Age over 75 y, in select patients (category 2B)
    - Individualize the decision to test beyond 75 years, with a discussion of the risks and benefits based on comorbidity status and estimated life expectancy
    - Test after 75 years only in very healthy people with little or no comorbidity, especially those who have never undergone PSA testing or have a rising PSA
    - Widespread testing in this population would substantially increase rates of overdetection and is not recommended
- Early detection is recommended starting at age 45 years, or 40 years if risk factors, through 75 years
    - In those who might have a life expectancy of at least 10 years
    - And who opt to participate in an early detection program
- Transgender women and other gender-diverse individuals assigned male at birth retain their prostates regardless of gender-affirmation surgery, and should undergo shared decision-making regarding age-appropriate prostate cancer early detection

# Early detection evaluation

- Baseline PSA
    - The best evidence supports the use of serum PSA for the early detection of prostate cancer
- Consider baseline digital rectal examination (DRE)
    - DRE should not be used as a stand-alone test
    - DRE can be considered as a baseline test in addition to serum PSA in all patients, but has its greatest usefulness in those with elevated PSA
    - Consider referral for biopsy or further testing if DRE is suspicious for cancer at any PSA level
- Interpreting the PSA value
    - Correct for 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors
    - For patients on gender-affirming hormones such as estrogen, interpret PSA cautiously, bearing in mind the castrating effects that may drastically decrease PSA levels
    - One study found a median PSA of 0.02 ng/mL in transgender women of median age 60 years on estrogen for at least 6 months with no known prostate cancer
    - Pay special attention to rising PSA levels in this population

# Result and follow-up interval

- PSA below 1 ng/mL, DRE normal (if done)
    - Repeat testing at 2- to 4-year intervals
    - Individuals 60 years and older with a PSA below 1.0 ng/mL have a very low risk of prostate cancer metastases in their lifetime
    - The interval ranges were not a unanimous Panel decision; some Panel members believe 2 to 4 years is too long for individuals aged 40 to 75 years with PSA above 1.0 ng/mL, but the Panel majority supported the intervals as listed
- PSA 1 to 3 ng/mL and DRE normal (if done)
    - Repeat testing at 1- to 2-year intervals
    - For younger patients, consider further evaluation (PROSD-3)
- PSA 3 ng/mL or less and DRE normal (if done)
    - Repeat testing at 1- to 2-year intervals
    - For younger patients, consider further evaluation (PROSD-3)
- PSA above 3 ng/mL and/or very suspicious DRE
    - Further evaluation and indications for biopsy (PROSD-3)
- A PSA above the median for the age group is itself a reason to consider further evaluation (PROSD-3), even without meeting the threshold of above 3 ng/mL
    - Median PSA for ages 40 to 49 years ranges from 0.5 to 0.7 ng/mL, and the 75th percentile from 0.7 to 0.9 ng/mL
    - The higher above the median, the greater the risk of prostate cancer and of aggressive prostate cancer

# Result and follow-up interval when testing beyond 75 years

- PSA below 4 ng/mL, DRE normal (if done), and no other indications for biopsy
    - Consider discontinuing screening if clinically appropriate, or
    - Repeat testing at 1- to 3-year intervals
    - Those over 75 years of age with a PSA below 3.0 ng/mL have a very low risk of prostate cancer metastases in their lifetime
- PSA 4 ng/mL or higher, or very suspicious DRE
    - Further evaluation and indications for biopsy (PROSD-3)
- Not screened
